# A QR Code Does Not Expire. The Service Behind It Might.

There is one sentence I keep seeing: “This QR code never expires.”

It sounds reassuring. It is also a little too easy.

A printed QR code has no battery, subscription, or hidden expiration date. If the pattern is still readable, a phone can scan it. That part is straightforward.

But the scan has to lead somewhere.

That is where the real question begins. The QR code may open a hosted page, a redirect, or a digital business card managed by a company. That address depends on a domain, a server, an account, and an organization willing and able to keep the whole thing running.

The printed pattern might last twenty years. Nobody can honestly promise the same for the service behind it without explaining how.

## I wanted to find out what “free” actually meant

I did not want to write another comparison built from homepage slogans. I opened the official product pages, pricing pages, help centers, and terms of fourteen digital business card and related QR services instead.

The review was carried out on 8 August 2026.

For each service, I wrote down a few practical facts:

- Can an individual create a usable card without paying?
- How many cards are included?
- Does the recipient need an app?
- Can the details change after the QR code has been printed?
- Is there a clear permanence claim?
- What limits sit behind the free offer?

The first finding was simple: free cards are now common, but “free” is not a standard product. It may mean one card, two cards, or four. It may limit certain analytics views, cap scans, restrict branding, or move the features teams actually need into a paid plan.

None of that is automatically unreasonable. It just needs to be stated clearly.

## “Permanent” is not a standard promise either

Wave says its dynamic QR code never expires. HiHello says a printed QR will keep linking to the card, while noting elsewhere that the card must remain active. ShareEcard uses the words permanent link. Lynkle says the card never expires.

QR TIGER documents a lifetime free QR plan, but each of its three free dynamic codes is limited to 500 scans. The boundary between that plan and full vCard access was not equally clear in the pricing material I reviewed.

Other services explain very clearly that users can update their details without reprinting the QR code, but do not make an equally explicit promise that the card or service will remain active forever.

That is not proof that those codes expire. It means I could not find an official source supporting the stronger claim. In the dataset, “not found” stays “not found.” I do not turn it into yes or no simply to fill a cell.

## Contradictions are worth keeping

Some vendors contradict themselves.

V1CE presents a free one-card plan and uses “free forever” language. Other official pages promote seven-day or thirty-day trials for broader features.

Cardyvo showed different free-card counts on different pages. Yoyo referred both to a free tier and a paid unlimited offer. Popl’s terms allow the company to change or discontinue its free plan.

I could have selected the most flattering version, or the version that made the comparison tidier. I did neither. Both claims remain visible in the data.

An official contradiction is not noise to clean up. It is useful information before somebody prints five hundred cards.

## What I would ask before printing

If I were sending a hosted QR code to a printer today, I would ask more than “Is it free?”

I would also ask:

- Is the address inside the QR code assigned to me for the long term?
- Can I change its content without changing the printed pattern?
- What happens to my first card if I stop paying?
- Are additional cards deleted or simply paused?
- Is there a scan limit?
- Can I export my QR code and contact details?
- What company or nonprofit actually operates the service?

That final question matters. A legal address, enterprise number, public terms, and privacy policy do not make a service immortal. They do, however, attach a visible operator to the promise.

## Where GetQRcard fits

I should be direct about this: I founded GetQRcard, and GetQRcard is included in the study. Pretending to be a completely detached reviewer would be absurd, so the relationship is disclosed throughout the project.

GetQRcard is published in Liège by Le Chemin Numérique ASBL, registered in Belgium under enterprise number 1039.717.066. The free plan includes one editable card and its permanent QR address with no time limit. The €15 annual plan adds unlimited cards and bulk creation.

Scan events are retained without an automatic deletion period under both plans. Totals and breakdowns by device and country are shown from the beginning. Only the day-by-day chart is limited: to the most recent 30 days on the free plan and 90 days on the annual plan. IP addresses are not retained in plain text: they are hashed with a salt that changes every day, preventing the service from linking the same fingerprint across different days.

The nonprofit has no shareholders. Payments cover hosting, domains, email, backups, maintenance, and development first. According to our public impact page, any surplus supports Le Chemin Numérique’s practical work with primary schools in Wallonia.

Does nonprofit status guarantee that GetQRcard will still exist in fifty years? No. Nobody can honestly guarantee that.

What it changes is the incentive: there is no investor exit to prepare, no advertising model, and no shareholders waiting for a return. More importantly, the operator, legal address, directors, terms, and operating model are public and verifiable.

## Why publish the evidence instead of naming a winner?

I did not want to finish with “the best service is…” That kind of conclusion ages quickly and depends too much on what each person needs.

I chose to publish the observations, first-party links, verification date, contradictions, and method instead. Vendors can request a correction if they provide an official page supporting it. The dataset is licensed under CC BY 4.0 so journalists, researchers, and users can reuse it.

A permanence claim should not be three words under a sign-up button. It should be precise enough for somebody else to verify.

That is what this project is trying to provide.
